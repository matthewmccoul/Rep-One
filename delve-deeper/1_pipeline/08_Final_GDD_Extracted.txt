Field Response
Game title Delve Deeper (working title)
Genre and category Dark pulp fantasy dungeon crawler RPG
Target platform PC, single-player, online-only
Player role One player controls one active adventurer at a time in this demo, choosing their race,
starting class, and alignment. Other existing or newly created characters can be selected for
death recovery, not simultaneous party control. The planned full game remains single-player,
with a party of up to four characters.
Primary objective Reach and use the boss-guarded descent beyond the first dungeon floor.
Player experience An old-school first-person dungeon crawl in the style of the reference games below, of which
Delve Deeper is a spiritual successor and homage, with rich lore and a responsive world: a
deeper, more immersive example of the genre, using LLM tools in ways they have never
been used before.
Intended audience Primarily nostalgic gamers looking to recapture the feel these games had for them as
children. The game is also meant to be playable by people without that nostalgia, as a novel
LLM-powered RPG.
Reference games Phantasy Star; Shining in the Darkness; Mordor: The Depths of Dejenol.
High Concept
Delve Deeper is a first-person dungeon crawler demo in which one adventurer explores, fights, flees, negotiates,
and searches before returning to town to recover and advance. Gods, NPCs, and a faction assess the
adventurer’s recorded actions whenever the day advances, through a rest attempt or a Wait after death, changing
relationships and predefined responses. Local encounter rates also change at these daily updates according to
creatures killed, their type and/or alignment, and the area where the actions occurred. Even in the dark, someone
is always watching.
Premise
High in remote mountains at the edge of the kingdom, far from civilization, a small mining town has accidentally
broken into an enormous ancient dwarven mine. It was abandoned long ago and is filled with traps, forgotten
magics, countless strange creatures, and untold riches. No one knows how deep it goes, what lies at the bottom,
or what happened to its makers. Adventurers are just beginning to arrive, drawn by the chance to make their
fortunes.
Core Loop
Loop Delve into the dungeon, face challenges and get rewards, return to town to advance and recuperate, go
back into a dungeon changed by player actions.

Scope of This Demo
● One town with a shop, rest, healing, a seer, and character creation; one dungeon floor ending at a
boss-guarded descent.
● One active player-controlled adventurer at a time; four races, three classes, and three alignments;
point-buy stats, one starting class with more classes added later, levels, and skill points. Death recovery
can use a different existing or newly created character.
● Three monster types; three traps; four door types (plain, secret, locked, and stuck); one puzzle; at least
three spells, three weapons, three armor pieces, and three consumables.
● Three AI NPCs, three AI gods, and one AI faction with one boss. Rest-triggered daily relationships and
changing encounter rates make choices about whom to fight or avoid and where to explore matter on
later visits.
● The demo includes exploration, combat, searching, inventory, loot, trading, recovery, and saving.
Additional dungeon floors are outside this capstone build.
Demo Foundation and Ongoing Development
This GDD defines a playable first-floor demo, not the full scope of Delve Deeper. Its stated content counts are
bounded demo deliverables and examples of the systems and content patterns that the larger game will use,
rather than an exhaustive catalogue of the full game’s content or features.
Full-game party goal: one human player will control a party of up to four characters, not a multiplayer group of four
people. One character is actively controlled at a time. During free-movement exploration, the remaining party
members follow that character in single file. When combat begins, adventurers occupy fixed positions in the room
until combat ends; free movement then resumes. Switching the first-person viewpoint selects the character whose
target and action the player can set. Unselected characters continue their last assigned action until told otherwise:
for example, swing an axe, cast fireball, hide, or block. If a targeted enemy is gone, they continue the assigned
action against the next available enemy. Actions such as blocking do not necessarily require a target. These
party-following and persistent-order systems are future full-game goals, not part of the single-adventurer demo
deliverable. Combat unfolds in real time, with assigned actions governed by timers rather than alternating turns.
Full-game story: As adventurers delve deeper, they will discover that the ancient dwarves were not the mine’s
makers. Like the town today, the dwarves were a wave of newcomers who colonized the mines to study them.
Deeper floors reveal clues to the dwarves’ fate, along with many more mysteries. The demo presents only the
surface premise.
The demo establishes the foundational gameplay systems, design principles, and reusable agentic development
workflow. The workflow used to produce the first floor is intended to be run again with new content briefs to create
the second and later floors, alongside other additions, in the established pattern. This is an AI-assisted
development process, not a commitment to automatic in-game generation.
Delve Deeper is intended to evolve through regular new content. A future version 1.0 is a release milestone, not
the end of development or a permanently finished content set. Broader full-game content and goals are outside
the course demo’s deliverables; later additions remain subject to human direction, review, and integration.
Planned paid expansions — custom side-dungeons: As part of ongoing content, multi-agent AI development
workflows will make custom, one-of-a-kind side-dungeons for players. Different agents will develop a concept,
draw a map, create new creatures, items, and traps, populate the side-dungeon, evaluate balance, and check that
the content is playable. Because each side-dungeon is one of a kind, it cannot be looked up online. This brings
Multi-Agent AI for Game Development | Assignment 02back the sense of mystery and exploration lost since the explosion of the internet, and brings it for the first time to
younger players who may never have experienced it. Side-dungeons are paid expansion content. After purchase,
a portal opens somewhere difficulty-appropriate in the dungeon. In lore, each portal leads to another remote part
of the mines, or another location belonging to the same original makers. Mechanically, each side-dungeon is a
small, isolated side-map connected to the main map only by its single portal. Expansions give players reasons to
keep spending after the initial game without microtransactions or pay-to-win mechanics that could cheapen the
game’s integrity.

Multi-Agent AI for Game Development | Assignment 02Systems, Resources, and Progression
Systems
● Character creation and advancement: Point-buy creation in town with race, alignment, stats, and a starting
class; leveling, skill points, and additional classes later. Only one adventurer is active at a time; the player may
select an existing character or create one to attempt body recovery after another character dies. Details are
under Character Creation and Advancement.
● Exploration and combat: Move freely in first person outside combat, reveal the minimap, and search rooms or
objects. Combat is real-time fighting with character stats, weapons, armor, and spells: when it begins, the
adventurer occupies a fixed position in the room until it ends, after which free movement resumes. Character
actions run on timers rather than alternating turns; exact action timings remain to be tuned. Full-game party
movement and orders are described under Demo Foundation and Ongoing Development.
● Encounters: Creatures, traps, doors, and a puzzle; choices and outcomes are described under Encounter
Choices and Outcomes. At each daily update, recorded kills temporarily reduce local spawn odds for matching
creature types and/or alignments. Reduced odds recover by a percentage per day toward their normal rates;
visiting alone neither reduces rates nor interrupts recovery. Further qualifying actions can change them.
Suppression duration, reduction amounts, and recovery percentages remain to be specified.
● Inventory and equipment: The adventurer’s inventory, equipment, consumables, and hotbar.
● Loot and trading: Loot, the shop, haggling, and town services are described under Loot, Resources, and Town
Services.
● AI relationships: Daily assessments of player actions produce relationship scores that select authored
responses, as described under Watchers and Town Characters and Assessment Consequences.
● Recovery and death: Rest in town always succeeds; dungeon rest can be interrupted by an ambush. Only
successful rest restores fatigue, heals 10 + Constitution HP up to maximum HP, grants pending levels, and lifts
the XP cap; an interrupted rest provides none of those benefits. Town healing services remain available. After
death, the player chooses Wait or Switch, as described under Death and Persistent World State. Around ten
days on the first floor versus years on much deeper future floors illustrates the intended Wait scaling, not fixed
balance values; only the first floor is in this demo.
● Time: A rest attempt advances the calendar by one in-game day; entering town or ordinary exploration alone
does not. Both successful and ambushed rests advance the day and trigger daily AI relationship assessments
and encounter-rate adjustments. Only successful rest grants recovery and advancement benefits. Choosing
Wait after death is a separate time-advancement case: a floor-dependent recovery delay, with random
variation, passes before the body is found and returned for revival. AI assessments and encounter updates run
once for the whole waiting interval, taking its length into account. Real-time combat timers are separate from
calendar advancement.

Progression
● Character progression: Surviving encounters—including traps and searches—earns XP, with some resolutions
awarding more than others. XP gains stop at one point below the cumulative threshold for the second level
above the selected class’s current level until a successful rest applies the pending level-up. For example, if
level 2 requires 150 total XP and level 3 requires 500, a level-1 class stops earning XP at 499 until successfully
resting; these numbers illustrate the rule rather than define the final XP table. Leveling grants class-dependent
rewards, such as spells, equipment proficiencies, action bonuses, and increased HP and mana, plus a skill
point to spend where the player chooses. Characters can take additional classes when they meet
requirements: stats, race, and alignment.
● Equipment progression: Obtain gear through monster and chest loot and shop trading to improve the
adventurer’s equipment.
● Relationship progression: Player actions change divine favor and NPC/faction relationships, affecting loot and
how others respond to the adventurer.
Multi-Agent AI for Game Development | Assignment 02● Dungeon progression: Advance toward the boss-guarded descent beyond the first floor, where the demo ends.
The adventurer’s actions change local encounter rates at rest-triggered daily updates; suppressed rates
gradually recover by a percentage per day, including on days when the area is visited without further qualifying
actions.
Encounter Choices and Outcomes
● Favor and hostility: Each monster group, including the faction, has a favor score that sets its hostility status on
a five-stage scale: hostile (−2, attacks on sight), threatening (−1), neutral (0), friendly (+1), and allied (+2).
● Fight: Real-time combat, as described under Combat. Defeating a group earns its full XP, and its loot comes
from authored drop tables modified by divine favor.
● Flee: Each enemy gets one free attack on the fleeing adventurer. A successful flee leaves the adventurer
wherever they moved while fleeing. Fleeing fails only if the adventurer cannot act, for example when paralyzed
or stunned. Fleeing grants very low XP.
● Negotiate: Possible outcomes include improved favor, information about the dungeon, or a group agreeing to
sell or give up its loot. Success is decided by a negotiation formula, to be developed, using level, Charisma,
Wisdom, Intelligence, gifts, and the group’s preferences (for example, “loves gold, hates weapons” or “inclined
to trade, not inclined to give out information”). Higher favor makes every other negotiation more likely to
succeed. Improving favor is the hardest outcome and often requires gold, items, equipment, or quests, such as
slaying a monster of an opposed alignment. Failure lowers favor according to its severity and can close off
options until favor improves. Negotiation can also influence faction standing.
● Negotiated loot: Persuading a group to give up its loot outright yields the same loot as fighting, but is difficult
even with friendly groups and lowers favor. Trading or buying is much easier and does not lower favor, but
yields less net loot. Friendlier groups offer better prices and trades; less favorable groups offer worse ones.
● Negotiation XP: Raising a group’s stage grants XP, and so do other negotiated outcomes, such as loot or help.
Each stage change grants XP only once per group. The negotiation path is meant to yield more XP than
combat. Specific values are TBD in balancing, set by the development team through prototyping and
playtesting.
● The faction: The demo’s one faction is a crew of bandits whose leader is the boss. They begin threatening, turn
hostile if attacked, robbed, or repeatedly offended, and respond well to gifts and quests. They are greedy and
morally ambiguous, so killing a few of the crew is not necessarily permanent if the adventurer later proves a
useful partner. Befriending individual bandits can influence the faction’s daily assessment of the adventurer,
and the bandit leader’s own favor also factors into that assessment.
● Search: A search formula, to be developed, using Intelligence, Wisdom, level, and class, determines whether
searching an object or room reveals hidden features.
● Traps: A found trap can be disarmed or avoided. A triggered trap goes off.
● Doors: Plain doors are ordinary doors. Locked doors must be picked, unlocked with magic, or broken. Stuck
doors must be broken. Secret doors can be plain, locked, or stuck, and must first be found by searching or with
magic.
● Puzzle: One puzzle, to be designed by the development team.
Character Creation and Advancement
● Stats: Strength, Intelligence, Wisdom, Constitution, Charisma, and Dexterity. Players buy starting stats with
points at creation.
● Races: Human, Elf, Dwarf, and Yeti. Race sets starting stat minimums and maximums, final stat maximums,
class eligibility, and resistances.
● Alignments: Good, Neutral, and Evil.
● Classes: Warrior, Thief, and Mage. Each class requires minimum stats, an eligible race, and an eligible
alignment; specific requirements are TBD in testing. A new character selects one eligible class at creation;
more classes can be added later once their requirements are met.
● Earning XP: When resting, the player may select which of the character’s classes earns XP until the next rest.
A new character starts with their creation class selected. Each class keeps its own XP total, which determines
its level on that class’s progression table; the character also shows a combined XP total across all classes.
● Leveling: Each class’s progression table sets the XP needed for each level; having several classes does not
change those requirements. On a successful rest, the selected class gains a level if its XP is enough. The XP
cap applies to the selected class: its XP stops one point below the threshold for its second level above its
current level until a successful rest.
● Skill points: Each level grants one skill point, spent on any of the six stats, up to the race’s final stat maximums.
● Multiple classes: For each class-derived value, such as HP, mana, bonuses, and proficiencies, a character
uses the highest value any of their classes provides at its current level. For example, a Warrior 2/Mage 2 has a
Multi-Agent AI for Game Development | Assignment 02Warrior’s HP and a Mage’s mana, while a Mage 9/Warrior 2 may instead have higher HP from the Mage class
because of the Mage’s much higher level.
● Demo range: Levels 1–10 on the first floor.
Loot, Resources, and Town Services
● Currency: Gold.
● Loot: Monsters and chests drop loot from authored drop tables modified by divine favor. Monster groups can
also sell, trade, or give up loot through negotiation.
● Shop: The shop sells basic gear and consumables and buys items. Prices are TBD in testing and balancing.
● Haggling: Once per in-game day, the player may attempt to negotiate better buy and sell rates, using stats,
roles, and the shopkeeper’s latest AI assessment. Rate changes are TBD in testing and balancing.
● Healer: For gold, a healer instantly heals all afflictions. Rates are TBD in testing and balancing.
● Revival: The same healer revives a returned body for gold. Each revival carries a small chance of losing one
Constitution point and a very slim chance of permanent death; both percentages are TBD.
● Consumables: Healing potions, mana crystals, and magic keys. A magic key opens one lock, once, so
characters who cannot pick locks or unlock them with magic can still make their way through the game.
Death and Persistent World State
● Death: When the active adventurer dies, the player chooses Wait or Switch, as described under Win and Loss
Conditions. The dead adventurer’s gear and gold stay with the body.
● Per-character relationships: Relationships with gods, NPCs, the faction, and monster groups are held
separately for each character in a world. For example, the evil god can regard three characters in the same
world differently.
● Shared world state: Map discovery is shared by all characters in a world. Encounter-rate changes also persist:
monster numbers and behavior reflect what adventurers have done in the dungeon over time, not which
character is currently delving.
● Waiting: Choosing Wait triggers a single daily update for the whole wait, which takes its length into account. A
one-day absence and a 30-day absence are both reported. For example, if the bandit leader asked the
adventurer to slay a monster that is still alive and has not seen them in 30 days, that report can affect his
judgment.
● Backups: Restoring a backup is mainly meant to recover from catastrophic glitches, though players can use it
otherwise. The development team will make backing up and restoring deliberately clunky to access, to
discourage their use as quick saves.
Watchers and Town Characters
● AI watchers: Seven characters receive daily LLM assessments: three NPCs, three gods, and the bandit faction.
Each keeps a separate relationship with each character in a world.
● Shopkeeper (AI NPC, loosely Neutral): Runs the shop and informs haggling as described above. The
shopkeeper’s favor rises when the adventurer sells low, buys high, makes big purchases, or sells him new,
rare, or valuable items.
● Beggar (AI NPC, loosely Good): Asks for coin and can be asked for rumors, chosen at random, such as where
monsters or items are, or the adventurer’s standing with the gods. Rumors become more accurate and more
helpful the more the beggar likes the adventurer.
● Mysterious Figure (AI NPC, loosely Evil): A pair of glowing eyes behind the bars of a sewer drain, with lanky
green arms and pointed fingers. He asks the adventurer to do things for him, such as slay a monster, bring him
an item, or go to specific coordinates and return to report what was there, and never reveals what these tasks
are for. Usually he gives no reward, saying there is still much work to be done; rarely, he rewards the
adventurer with rare items.
● NPCs and their gods: The adventurer’s standing with each NPC affects, but does not define, the opinion of the
god of that NPC’s alignment.
● Gods (AI): A Good god, a Neutral god, and an Evil god. Their identities are to be developed by the AI
development team. Divine favor modifies loot as described above.
● The bandits (AI faction): Led by the boss, the bandits have taken to extorting people going in and out of the
mine. They are morally grey and self-serving, but not actively malicious or murderous. They respond especially
well to gifts; their other reactions to the adventurer are described under Encounter Choices and Outcomes.
● Seer (not AI): For gold, the seer has a chance to tell the adventurer approximate information, such as where
monsters, items, or bodies currently are, give or take, and the adventurer’s general standing with a god or a
faction.
● Healer (not AI): The healer is intentionally not an AI watcher, so they heal anyone, regardless of standing.
Multi-Agent AI for Game Development | Assignment 02Assessment Consequences
● Score bands: Each assessment returns a 0–100 relationship score, which maps to the five stages in even
bands of 20: hostile (0–19), threatening (20–39), neutral (40–59), friendly (60–79), and allied (80–100).
● Gods: A god’s favor or disfavor can temporarily modify drop rates, stat bonuses and penalties, and success
rates on checks such as haggling, negotiation, and searching. Gods can also place enemies of their choosing
in places of their choosing, within reason, and put specific items in the possession of specific creatures in the
dungeon. Gods choose these interventions from authored options within authored limits; the LLM does not
invent new mechanics. The full range of divine actions and how much influence gods have are TBD in
development and testing.
● NPCs: Standing changes the tone and content of NPC dialogue, which can also reflect how others regard the
adventurer. For example, if the Evil god truly hates the adventurer, the beggar may say, “I’ll bet ____ curses
your name,” or “They say ____ checks for you under his bed at night.”
● Alignment: The adventurer’s alignment affects the gods’ opinions of them.
Boss Encounter
● Boss and standing: The bandit boss guards the descent and starts at threatening, demanding a toll to let the
adventurer pass. At neutral and friendly the toll is lower; at allied he lets the adventurer through; at hostile he
attacks on sight.
● Fighting the bandits: Bandits present when the adventurer attacks become hostile, because they have been
attacked. The bandit faction as a whole decides how to respond during its next daily assessment.
● Winning: Going down the descent wins the demo, however the adventurer got past the boss.

Runtime AI: Daily Relationships
Whenever the in-game day advances, through a rest attempt or a Wait after death, relevant action counts are sent
separately to the three NPCs, three gods, and one faction: seven assessments. This happens after both
successful and ambushed rest attempts. Each receives its profile, prior relationship, and a compact record of
relevant actions, and returns one short assessment plus a relationship percentage. Only the active character is
assessed. The boss belongs to the faction; it does not require an eighth assessor.
● Gods: favor or disfavor can temporarily modify loot, stats, check success rates, and enemy and item
placement, as described under Assessment Consequences.
● NPCs: their attitudes, dialogue, and interactions reflect their assessment; the shopkeeper’s latest
assessment also informs haggling.
● Faction: its relationship selects predefined behavior, such as allied, friendly, neutral, threatening, or
hostile; later actions can improve or worsen it.
The game applies validated scores through fixed rules. For example, a hostile threshold can make previously
neutral creatures attack on a later visit. Combat actions and daily encounter-rate adjustments stay in ordinary
game code. Daily changes are communicated through demeanor and dialogue.

04 Technical Strategy
Technical Choice Plan
Engine or framework Unreal Engine, using reusable gameplay components and data-driven rules to support
the first-person 3D view, real-time timer-driven combat, single-adventurer systems, and
rest-triggered daily AI updates.
Target platform PC, single-player, online-only. Demo: one active adventurer. Planned full game: one
player controls a party of up to four characters, not four human players.
Primary AI tools or models Claude Pro models and ChatGPT/Codex support design, engineering, review, and
analysis; Galaxy.AI provides additional model access. Meshy supports occasional asset
creation. Runtime assessments use a fallback ladder of five progressively lower-cost
models; DeepSeek is the initial candidate, Qwen, GLM, and MiMo are candidates for
the other rungs, and the fifth model is TBD. Model choice is checked against response
quality, latency, and usage.
Existing assets or plugins Primarily premade models, materials, animations, music, sound, and suitable plugins
from Humble Bundle and Bundlefoundry. The Asset Curator checks style, compatibility,
and source records; the Software Engineer handles integration.
Saving and loading Autosave runs continuously and preserves character progress, inventory, map
discovery, day, action counters, encounter state, and relationships. If the game closes
during a daily update, completed assessments are kept and are not rerun; unfinished
assessments resume on loading where they left off on the five-model fallback ladder
until one succeeds. Backups can be created and restored, returning the world to the
backup’s state; they are mainly for recovering from catastrophic glitches and are
deliberately clunky to access.
